# CPU & Motherboard Pairing

The CPU acts as your PC's brain, handling game logic and background tasks, while the motherboard is the nervous system that connects every component together. Because they must physically and electronically match, you typically choose or upgrade them as a pair.

## Socket & Chipset Compatibility
Every processor fits a specific physical socket, such as **AM5** for modern AMD Ryzen chips or **LGA 1851** for current Intel Core Ultra chips. Moving to a newer socket design always requires a matching motherboard. 

Your motherboard's **chipset** determines its expansion features and tuning options:
* **Mid-Range (B650 / B850):** Covers everything most gamers need, including PCIe 5.0 support and solid power delivery without extra cost.
* **Enthusiast (X870 / Z890):** Adds USB4 ports, [[ram-nvme-storage|extra M.2 slots]], and heavier cooling for extreme overclocking.
### Motherboard Form Factors
Motherboards come in three main sizes that dictate case compatibility and expansion room. **ATX** is the standard full size, offering four RAM slots, multiple M.2 slots, and easy cable access. **Micro-ATX (mATX)** trims a couple of inches off the bottom, keeping full performance in a smaller, budget-friendly footprint. **Mini-ITX** is built for ultra-compact rigs, limiting you to two RAM slots, one GPU slot, and [[pc-cooling-airflow|tight cooler clearance]].

## Gaming vs. Productivity Specs
For gaming, fast single-core speeds and large on-chip cache matter far more than high core counts. CPUs with **3D V-Cache** (like AMD's X3D lineup) stack extra memory directly onto the chip for major frame rate boosts in CPU-heavy games. If you regularly stream or edit video while gaming, step up to a 12- or 16-core chip to keep multitasking smooth.
